### **Introduction: The Role of Filtering and Observation in LCI Control**

The control algorithm of an LCI relies on accurate, real-time feedback of the system's electrical state variables (voltages, currents, frequency, and rotor position). However, raw measurements are invariably corrupted by:
1.  **Switching Harmonics:** Generated by the 6-pulse or 12-pulse thyristor bridges.
2.  **Commutation Notches:** Voltage distortions caused by the finite time it takes for current to commutate from one thyristor to the next.
3.  **Background Grid/Machine Distortion:** Pre-existing harmonics in the power system or non-sinusoidal back-EMF from the machine.
4.  **Measurement Noise:** High-frequency noise from sensors and analog-to-digital converters (ADCs).

A well-designed **filtering chain** cleans these signals, while **observers** estimate critical state variables that are either not measured directly (like grid phase angle) or are difficult to measure reliably (like rotor position at low speeds). This front-end signal processing block provides clean, stable inputs to the core control loops (e.g., DC link current control, speed control), ensuring system stability and performance.

### **Part 1: Signal Filtering Chain: Requirements and Solutions**

The primary goal of the filtering chain is to extract the fundamental component of AC signals and the average value of DC signals while minimizing phase delay, which can compromise control loop stability.

#### **General Requirements for Digital Filters**
*   **Harmonic Attenuation:** The filter must effectively suppress the characteristic harmonics of the converter (typically $6k \pm 1$ or $12k \pm 1$ times the fundamental frequency) and high-frequency noise.
*   **Phase Lag Minimization:** Every filter introduces a phase delay. This delay must be minimized and accounted for, especially in fast-acting control loops. Excessive phase lag can lead to instability.
*   **Bandwidth:** The filter must pass the fundamental component of the signal with unity gain and minimal phase shift. For variable-speed applications like a GTS, the filter's characteristics may need to adapt to the changing fundamental frequency.
*   **Computational Efficiency:** The algorithms must be simple enough to execute within the controller's sampling period (typically 50-200 µs).

---

#### **Signal-Specific Filtering Solutions**

**1. Grid-Side AC Voltages ($V_{grid}$)**
*   **Purpose:** Essential input for the grid-side Phase-Locked Loop (PLL) to determine grid frequency and phase angle. This angle is the reference for the rectifier firing angle, $\alpha$.
*   **Challenges:** Grid voltage can have background distortion and high-frequency noise. Commutation notches from other loads can also be present.
*   **Solution: Low-Pass Filter (LPF)**
    *   A **Second-Order Butterworth LPF** is a standard choice. It offers a good trade-off between attenuation and phase linearity in the passband.
    *   The cutoff frequency ($f_c$) should be set well above the fundamental (e.g., 400-800 Hz for a 50/60 Hz grid) to minimize phase lag at the fundamental frequency while still filtering higher-order harmonics and noise.
    *   For applications demanding minimal phase distortion, a **Bessel filter** can be used, though its attenuation (roll-off) is less sharp than a Butterworth filter.

**2. Machine-Side AC Voltages ($V_{mach}$)**
*   **Purpose:** Used to estimate the machine's back-EMF, which is crucial for calculating the required inverter extinction angle ($\gamma$) and for sensorless position/speed estimation.
*   **Challenges:** The voltage waveform is heavily distorted with deep commutation notches caused by the inverter's operation. The fundamental frequency varies with machine speed.
*   **Solution: Adaptive Low-Pass Filter**
    *   A standard LPF is used, but its parameters must adapt to the machine's speed.
    *   **Requirement:** The cutoff frequency should be scaled with the estimated machine frequency ($\omega_m$): $f_c = k \cdot f_m$, where $k$ is a constant (e.g., $k \in [5, 10]$).
    *   This ensures that the filter provides adequate harmonic rejection at high speeds without attenuating the fundamental component at low speeds.

**3. DC Link Current ($I_d$)**
*   **Purpose:** This is the most critical feedback signal for the LCI's primary control loop. The controller adjusts the rectifier firing angle $\alpha$ to regulate $I_d$ to its reference value.
*   **Challenges:** The DC link current is not pure DC. It contains a significant ripple component at $6f$ or $12f$ (where $f$ is the line frequency). Filtering this signal with a simple LPF would introduce a significant delay, destabilizing the current control loop.
*   **Solution: Notch Filter + LPF**
    *   The optimal solution is to use a **digital notch filter** precisely tuned to the dominant ripple frequency (e.g., 300/360 Hz for a 6-pulse system; 600/720 Hz for a 12-pulse system).
    *   The notch filter selectively removes the specific ripple frequency with very little phase lag at other frequencies.
    *   The transfer function for a digital notch filter is:
        $$ H(z) = \frac{1 - 2\cos(\omega_0 T_s)z^{-1} + z^{-2}}{1 - 2r\cos(\omega_0 T_s)z^{-1} + r^2 z^{-2}} $$
        Where:
        *   $\omega_0$ is the ripple frequency to be rejected (in rad/s).
        *   $T_s$ is the controller sampling time.
        *   $r$ is the pole radius, which controls the notch width ($r$ is slightly less than 1, e.g., 0.95).
    *   A gentle, first-order LPF can be placed in series with the notch filter to remove any remaining high-frequency noise.

**4. Grid-Side and Machine-Side AC Currents ($I_{grid}, I_{mach}$)**
*   **Purpose:** Primarily used for protection (overcurrent) and monitoring. They can also be used for calculating power factor or verifying commutation success.
*   **Challenges:** These currents have a quasi-square waveform, rich in low-order harmonics ($5^{th}, 7^{th}, 11^{th}, 13^{th}$, etc.).
*   **Solution: Low-Pass Filter**
    *   A **Second-Order Butterworth LPF** is sufficient.
    *   Since these signals are not typically in a fast-acting control loop, a lower cutoff frequency can be used (e.g., 150-250 Hz) to achieve better filtering of the waveform harmonics to extract the fundamental component for measurement display or RMS calculation.

### **Part 2: Essential Observers**

Observers are algorithms that use the filtered measurements and a model of the system to estimate unmeasured states.

**1. Grid-Side Observer: Phase-Locked Loop (PLL)**
*   **Purpose:** To track the grid voltage's phase angle ($\theta_{grid}$) and frequency ($\omega_{grid}$) in real-time. The estimated angle $\hat{\theta}_{grid}$ is the absolute foundation for the rectifier's timing control.
*   **Requirement:** Must be robust to grid voltage distortion, sags, and frequency variations.
*   **Solution: Synchronous Reference Frame PLL (SRF-PLL)**
    *   This is the industry-standard observer for grid synchronization. Its operation is as follows:
        1.  **Transformation:** The filtered three-phase grid voltages ($V_a, V_b, V_c$) are transformed into a rotating reference frame (dq-frame) using the Park transform and the PLL's own estimated angle, $\hat{\theta}_{grid}$.
            $$ \begin{bmatrix} V_d \\ V_q \end{bmatrix} = \frac{2}{3} \begin{bmatrix} \cos(\hat{\theta}_{grid}) & \cos(\hat{\theta}_{grid}-\frac{2\pi}{3}) & \cos(\hat{\theta}_{grid}+\frac{2\pi}{3}) \\ -\sin(\hat{\theta}_{grid}) & -\sin(\hat{\theta}_{grid}-\frac{2\pi}{3}) & -\sin(\hat{\theta}_{grid}+\frac{2\pi}{3}) \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} V_a \\ V_b \\ V_c \end{bmatrix} $$
        2.  **Control Loop:** In steady-state, if the dq-frame is perfectly aligned with the voltage vector, the q-axis voltage ($V_q$) will be zero. A Proportional-Integral (PI) controller is used to regulate $V_q$ to zero.
        3.  **Estimation:** The output of the PI controller is the estimated grid frequency deviation. This is added to the nominal frequency to get the final frequency estimate, $\hat{\omega}_{grid}$.
        4.  **Integration:** The estimated frequency is integrated to produce the estimated angle: $\hat{\theta}_{grid}(t) = \int \hat{\omega}_{grid}(t) dt$. This angle is then fed back to the Park transform, closing the loop.

**2. Machine-Side Observer: Rotor Position and Speed Estimator**
*   **Purpose:** To determine the synchronous machine's rotor position ($\theta_{rotor}$) and speed ($\omega_{rotor}$). This is required to correctly time the inverter firing pulses (controlled via angle $\beta$ or $\gamma$) to ensure successful commutation and control machine torque.
*   **Requirement:** Must operate over a wide speed range, from near-zero to full speed.
*   **Solutions:**
    *   **Method A: Physical Sensor (Most Robust)**
        *   The most reliable method, especially for GTS applications, is to use a physical position sensor like a **resolver** or an **absolute encoder**.
        *   This directly provides the rotor position, eliminating the complexities and failure modes of sensorless observers at low speed. The measured speed can be obtained by differentiating the position signal and then low-pass filtering the result.
    *   **Method B: Sensorless Observer (Back-EMF Based)**
        *   This is feasible once the machine speed is high enough (typically > 5-10% of rated speed) for the back-EMF to be significant and measurable.
        *   The observer uses the machine's voltage equations:
            $$ \vec{V}_{mach} = R_s \vec{I}_{mach} + L_s \frac{d\vec{I}_{mach}}{dt} + \vec{E}_{mach} $$
        *   The back-EMF vector, $\vec{E}_{mach}$, contains the position and speed information ($\vec{E}_{mach}$ is a function of $\omega_{rotor}$ and $\theta_{rotor}$).
        *   The observer estimates the back-EMF by calculating: $\hat{\vec{E}}_{mach} = \vec{V}_{mach} - R_s \vec{I}_{mach} - L_s \frac{d\vec{I}_{mach}}{dt}$.
        *   A PLL-like structure can then be used to lock onto the phase of the estimated back-EMF vector $\hat{\vec{E}}_{mach}$, yielding estimates for rotor position and speed.
        *   **Crucial Limitation:** This method fails at zero and very low speeds because $\vec{E}_{mach} \approx 0$.

### **Part 3: Special Considerations for Gas Turbine Starter (GTS) Applications**

GTS applications impose unique and severe operating conditions that must be addressed in the filtering and observation design.

**1. The Zero-Speed Problem**
*   **Challenge:** An LCI is a "line-commutated" device, meaning it relies on the AC source voltage (the machine's back-EMF on the inverter side) to turn off the thyristors. At zero or very low speeds, there is no back-EMF, so natural commutation is impossible.
*   **Solution: Open-Loop Pulsed Mode Startup**
    *   For initial rotation (from 0 to ~10% speed), the LCI does not operate in its normal mode. Instead, it operates in a **forced commutation** or **pulsed current mode**.
    *   The control system fires specific thyristors to inject a DC current pulse into a chosen pair of stator windings for a short duration, creating a magnetic field that pulls the rotor into alignment.
    *   The controller then commutates the current to the next pair of windings at a very low, slowly increasing frequency. This forces the rotor to turn, behaving like a brushless DC motor.
    *   **Observer Impact:** In this mode, sensorless observers are inactive. The control is purely open-loop or relies on a physical position sensor to determine which thyristors to fire.

**2. Wide Speed and Frequency Range**
*   **Challenge:** The machine operates from 0 Hz to the grid frequency (50/60 Hz). As noted before, fixed-parameter filters are unsuitable.
*   **Solution: Adaptive Filtering and Observers**
    *   **Filtering:** The cutoff frequencies of all machine-side filters **must** be programmed to be a function of the estimated machine speed.
    *   **Observation:** The machine-side position observer (PLL) must have controller gains (P and I) that are scheduled with speed to maintain consistent performance across the entire operating range.

**3. Transition Handover**
*   **Challenge:** There must be a smooth transition from the open-loop pulsed mode to the normal, closed-loop, back-EMF commutated mode.
*   **Solution: Blending and Hysteresis**
    *   The control algorithm monitors the estimated back-EMF amplitude. When it crosses a reliable threshold (e.g., at 10% speed), the control strategy switches to the standard LCI mode using the back-EMF based observer or sensor.
    *   Hysteresis (different speed thresholds for entering and exiting the mode) should be built into the logic to prevent chattering between control modes if the speed fluctuates around the transition point.

In summary, a successful LCI control system for a GTS application requires an integrated, adaptive, and mode-switching design for its filtering and observation subsystems. The most robust industrial solutions often rely on physical position sensors to bypass the challenges of low-speed sensorless observation, combined with adaptive filtering and a special open-loop startup routine.
