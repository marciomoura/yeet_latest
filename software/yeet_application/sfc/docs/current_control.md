# Comprehensive Guide to the LCI Current Controller

## 1. Overview and Purpose

The current control loop is the core of a Line-Commutated Inverter (LCI) drive system. It is a high-speed, inner control loop responsible for directly manipulating the torque produced by the synchronous motor. Its robust design is critical for both high-performance LCI drives and soft-starter applications.

The primary objectives of the current controller are:

1.  **Fast and Accurate Torque Control:** Based on the fundamental relationship $T_e = K_t I_d$, controlling the DC link current ($I_d$) provides direct and rapid control over the motor\'s electromagnetic torque ($T_e$). This allows the outer speed control loop to effectively command acceleration or deceleration.
2.  **Overcurrent Protection:** By forcing the DC link current to follow a precisely limited reference, the loop inherently protects the motor, thyristors, and other power electronics from damaging overcurrent conditions during transients or faults.

This controller is the workhorse in a **cascaded control structure**, where it acts as the inner "slave" loop, receiving its command (the current reference) from an outer "master" loop, which is typically a speed controller.

## 2. Fundamental LCI Equations

A firm grasp of the steady-state LCI equations is essential to understand the design of the current controller.

#### **Assumptions:**
*   The synchronous motor has a constant field current, resulting in constant rotor flux ($\Phi$).
*   The DC link current ($I_d$) is continuous and well-smoothed by the large DC link inductor ($L_{dc}$).
*   Thyristors are treated as ideal switches.

#### **Rectifier Side (Source Converter)**
The average DC voltage produced by the 6-pulse rectifier is a function of its firing angle $\alpha$ and the DC link current $I_d$.
$$ V_d = V_{do} \cos(\alpha) - R_c I_d $$
where:
*   $V_d$ is the average DC voltage at the rectifier terminals.
*   $\alpha$ is the rectifier firing angle, the primary control variable for the current loop.
*   $V_{do} = \frac{3\sqrt{2}}{\pi}V_{LL,s}$ is the ideal no-load DC voltage. $V_{LL,s}$ is the RMS line-to-line voltage of the AC source. This value must be measured or known.
*   $R_c = \frac{3X_s}{\pi}$ represents the voltage drop due to source reactance $X_s$ during commutation.

#### **Inverter Side (Load Converter)**
The inverter, connected to the rotating synchronous motor, acts as a voltage source on the DC link. This back-EMF is a function of the motor speed and the inverter firing angle $\beta$.
$$ V_{di} = V_{dio} \cos(\beta) + R_{ci} I_d $$
where:
*   $V_{di}$ is the average DC back-EMF at the inverter terminals.
*   $\beta$ is the inverter firing angle.
*   $V_{dio} = \frac{3\sqrt{2}}{\pi}V_{LL,m}$ is the ideal no-load DC voltage generated by the motor\'s back-EMF. Since the motor\'s internal voltage $V_{LL,m}$ is proportional to speed, $V_{dio}$ is also directly proportional to motor speed.
*   $R_{ci} = \frac{3X_m}{\pi}$ represents the voltage drop due to motor reactance $X_m$ during commutation.

For safe thyristor turn-off (commutation), it is common to control the **extinction angle** $\gamma$. The relationship is:
$$ \beta = 180^\circ - \gamma $$
Substituting this into the inverter equation gives:
$$ V_{di} = -V_{dio} \cos(\gamma) + R_{ci} I_d $$
A minimum extinction angle ($\gamma > \gamma_{min}$) must be maintained for successful commutation.

#### **DC Link and Motor Torque**
The DC link inductor connects the two converters. The dynamics of the DC link current are described by:
$$ L_{dc} \frac{dI_d}{dt} = V_d - V_{di} $$
The control system manipulates $V_d$ (via $\alpha$) to drive the current $I_d$ to its desired value against the back-EMF $V_{di}$.

The most critical relationship for control is the motor\'s torque production. For a separately excited synchronous motor, the electromagnetic torque ($T_e$) is directly proportional to the DC link current:
$$ T_e = K_t I_d $$
where $K_t$ is the motor torque constant. **This equation establishes that by controlling the DC link current, we directly control the motor torque.**

The current controller is implemented as a series of functional blocks that work together to achieve fast and stable current regulation.

#### **Functional Blocks**

1.  **Current Limiter:**
    *   **Input:** The raw current reference ($I_{d,ref}$) from the outer loop.
    *   **Action:** Saturates the reference to enforce safe operational limits.
    *   **Output:** A limited, safe current reference ($I_{d,ref,limited}$).
    $$ I_{d,ref,limited} = \text{saturate}(I_{d,ref}, I_{d,min}, I_{d,max}) $$

2.  **Error Calculation:**
    *   **Inputs:** The limited reference ($I_{d,ref,limited}$) and the measured DC link current feedback ($I_d$).
    *   **Action:** Calculates the error signal $e_c$.
    *   **Output:** $e_c = I_{d,ref,limited} - I_d$.

3.  **PI Controller:**
    *   **Input:** The error signal $e_c$.
    *   **Action:** Processes the error to determine the required change in rectifier voltage. The proportional term provides a fast response, while the integral term eliminates steady-state error.
    *   **Output:** A corrective control voltage, $V_{ctrl,PI}$.

4.  **Feedforward Compensation:**
    *   **Input:** Measured DC link current ($I_d$) and other system parameters needed to estimate the inverter back-EMF.
    *   **Action:** Calculates an estimate of the inverter back-EMF, $V_{di,est}$, using the inverter state equation. This proactively cancels out the largest disturbance in the DC link.
    *   **Output:** Estimated back-EMF, $V_{di,est}$.

5.  **Total Control Voltage Calculation:**
    *   **Inputs:** The PI controller output ($V_{ctrl,PI}$) and the feedforward voltage ($V_{di,est}$).
    *   **Action:** Sums the two components.
    *   **Output:** The total desired rectifier voltage, $V_{ctrl,total} = V_{ctrl,PI} + V_{di,est}$.

6.  **Firing Angle Generation (Linearization):**
    *   **Input:** The total control voltage $V_{ctrl,total}$ and the measured source voltage $V_{do}$.
    *   **Action:** Inverts the rectifier equation using an `arccos` function to linearize the system. This converts the desired voltage into the required firing angle.
    *   **Output:** The final firing angle reference, $\alpha_{ref}$.
    $$ \alpha_{ref} = \arccos\left(\frac{V_{ctrl,total}}{V_{do}}\right) $$

## 4. Operational Modes

The source of the current reference ($I_{d,ref}$) depends on the system\'s operational mode.

#### **Mode 1: Normal Operation (Speed/Torque Control)**
*   **Context:** The motor is running and synchronized. The primary goal is to regulate speed based on a process demand.
*   **Current Reference Source:** The **outer speed control loop**. The speed controller compares the speed reference to the measured speed and generates the current reference ($I_{d,ref}$) as its output. This $I_{d,ref}$ is effectively a torque command.

#### **Mode 2: Soft-Starter Application (Startup)**
*   **Context:** The motor is at standstill or very low speed. The goal is to smoothly magnetize the motor and accelerate it to a minimum self-sustaining speed without causing high inrush currents or mechanical shock.
*   **Current Reference Source:** A **pre-defined startup profile or ramp generator**.
*   **Process:**
    1.  **Magnetization:** A current reference is slowly ramped up from zero to a nominal magnetizing level. The firing angle is controlled to build up current in the motor windings while the rotor is stationary.
    2.  **Breakaway Torque:** Once magnetized, the current is increased further to provide the torque needed to overcome static friction and start rotation.
    3.  **Acceleration:** The current reference is controlled according to a specific profile to accelerate the motor. During this phase, the inverter firing angle must also be controlled in coordination with the rotor position to ensure continuous torque production.

In both modes, the fundamental architecture of the current controller remains the same. The only change is the source providing the top-level current reference.
